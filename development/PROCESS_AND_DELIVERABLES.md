Updated: November 8, 2026. Current step: develop the idea through founder conversation.

## 1. Develop the idea

Use the earlier product-spec prompt as an interviewing method. Start from PROJECT_BRIEF.md and the sources below. Ask one question at a time, give two or three concrete examples, and update the brief from the answer.

Work through the first product promise, first customer, concrete need or desire, current alternatives, repeat-use interaction, founder motivation, evidence, practical constraints, business model, and first demonstration. Explain consequential tradeoffs without choosing the founder's priorities for them.

Completion means the founder can describe the customer, what that person does with the first product, why they return, and how that first product can support the larger vision. Unknowns can remain if they are labeled and paired with a way to investigate them.

## 2. Teach the concepts needed for the decisions

Use the supplied teaching prompt during actual tutoring, rather than delivering another prompt or a full lecture at once. The project and application goal are already known; do not restart with generic intake. Ask about current understanding and preferred teaching style when those remain unknown.

Each lesson targets one practical question, has three to five core ideas, and budgets twenty minutes including checks and the self-test. For example: big picture, one minute; three core blocks with checks, four minutes each; common misconception, two minutes; five-question self-test, five minutes. These are planned learning times, not a claim about elapsed chat time.

Teach one block, ask its quick check, and wait. Correct a misunderstanding briefly before continuing. At the end, ask five test questions one at a time, including at least two applications to a new situation. Keep the answers hidden until attempted and grade after all five answers. Give explanations, a score, what was omitted, and one next action. Record demonstrated understanding, not merely that text was shown.

Use real source material and the inspected skeleton as examples. Clearly identify proposed Madken designs and hypothetical exercises; do not present imagined hardware or pilot outcomes as observed examples.

Candidate lessons, selected after the first product decision:

| Lesson question | Decision it supports | Artifact it improves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What makes a character persist when its model or body changes? | Identity, memory, authored lore, model, and hardware boundaries | Product definition and character specification |
| What does the first customer actually want to do? | Launch promise, repeat use, and alternatives | Customer/problem statement and validation plan |
| What does local-first mean for this product? | Device, storage, model access, offline limits, and cost | Architecture and privacy requirements |
| When can a character act or share information? | Owner control, consent, tool access, and fictional versus real actions | Data and permission model |
| What is the smallest convincing demonstration? | Mockup versus simulation versus implemented feature | Prototype brief and honest demonstration outline |
| How can the first product support the business? | Revenue hypothesis, costs, manufacturing assumptions, and release scope | Business-model and roadmap drafts |

Legal and blockchain questions receive targeted lessons only if they become relevant. Verify current claims at that point. Do not make learning the entire six-year technology roadmap a prerequisite for preparing a truthful application.
